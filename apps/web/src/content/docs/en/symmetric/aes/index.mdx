## A public competition, a Belgian winner

In 1997 NIST asked the world for a successor to DES. Fifteen designs entered, five reached the
final round, and in October 2000 **Rijndael** by Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen won. NIST published it
as the **Advanced Encryption Standard** in FIPS 197 (November 2001).

Rijndael allows several block sizes; AES fixes the block at **128 bits** and offers three key sizes.
The key length decides the number of rounds `Nr` (FIPS 197, Table 3):

AES works on a 4×4 byte matrix, the **state**. Input bytes fill it **column by column**:
`s[r, c] = in[r + 4c]` (FIPS 197, §3.4). Below is the plaintext of FIPS 197, Appendix B.

## Four steps per round

Every round applies four transformations to the state (FIPS 197, §5.1):

- **SubBytes** replaces each byte through the S-box — the only non-linear step (§5.1.1).
- **ShiftRows** rotates row `r` left by `r` bytes (§5.1.2).
- **MixColumns** mixes the four bytes of each column (§5.1.3).
- **AddRoundKey** XORs a 16-byte round key into the state (§5.1.4).

An extra AddRoundKey comes first, and the last round skips MixColumns. That keeps encryption and
decryption structurally alike.

## What sits in memory

A running AES needs more than the 16-, 24- or 32-byte key: **KeyExpansion** expands it into the key schedule:
`Nr + 1` round keys — 176 bytes for AES-128, 240 bytes for AES-256. Those expanded round keys are
what memory forensics usually finds. The [Memory & hardware](../memory-and-hardware/) page shows
how CPUs and libraries lay them out.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection part="break">

## Why not fewer rounds?

Rounds are the safety margin. The *square* (integral) attack, published together with the cipher,
breaks 4 rounds with a few hundred chosen plaintexts and can be stretched to 6. The best published
attacks on AES-128 reach 7 rounds; against the full 10 rounds, the best known key recovery
(biclique, 2011) costs about 2¹²⁶ operations — barely below brute force.

</LessonSection>

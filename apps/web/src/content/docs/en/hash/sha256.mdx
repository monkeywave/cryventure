## Sixty-four rounds per block

The [hash functions page](../) treated the compression function as a box. Inside, SHA-256 copies the
chaining value into eight **working variables** `a … h`, expands the 16 words of the block into a
**message schedule** of 64 words `W₀ … W₆₃`, and runs **64 rounds**, one per word. Step 4 adds
`a … h` back to the chaining value: the feed-forward (FIPS 180-4, §6.2.2).

<KeyFacts>
  <KeyFact label="Word" value="32 bit" kind="state" />
  <KeyFact label="Working variables" value="8 words" kind="state" />
  <KeyFact label="Rounds" value="64" kind="constant" />
  <KeyFact label="Block" value="16 words" kind="plaintext" />
</KeyFacts>

<WhyBox constant="K₀ = 428a2f98" title="why the round constants look random">
  `K₀ … K₆₃` are the first 32 fractional bits of the cube roots of the first 64 primes
  (FIPS 180-4, §4.2.2): ∛2 = 1.**428a2f98**… in hex. Anyone can recompute them.
</WhyBox>

<WhyBox constant="H₀ = 6a09e667" title="where the initial hash value comes from">
  The SHA-256 IV takes the first 32 fractional bits of the square roots of the first 8 primes
  (FIPS 180-4, §5.3.3): √2 = 1.**6a09e667**… in hex.
</WhyBox>

## Four functions, one round

FIPS 180-4, §4.1.2 defines six functions on 32-bit words:

<Formula caption="SHA-256 functions, FIPS 180-4 §4.1.2 (ROTR = rotate right, SHR = shift right, ∧ = AND, ¬ = NOT).">
{`Ch(x, y, z)  = (x ∧ y) ⊕ (¬x ∧ z)                 x chooses between y and z
Maj(x, y, z) = (x ∧ y) ⊕ (x ∧ z) ⊕ (y ∧ z)       bitwise majority
Σ₀(x) = ROTR²(x)  ⊕ ROTR¹³(x) ⊕ ROTR²²(x)
Σ₁(x) = ROTR⁶(x)  ⊕ ROTR¹¹(x) ⊕ ROTR²⁵(x)
σ₀(x) = ROTR⁷(x)  ⊕ ROTR¹⁸(x) ⊕ SHR³(x)
σ₁(x) = ROTR¹⁷(x) ⊕ ROTR¹⁹(x) ⊕ SHR¹⁰(x)`}
</Formula>

<Formula caption="Schedule and round t, FIPS 180-4 §6.2.2 steps 1 and 3 (+ is addition mod 2³²).">
{`Wₜ = σ₁(Wₜ₋₂) + Wₜ₋₇ + σ₀(Wₜ₋₁₅) + Wₜ₋₁₆      16 ≤ t ≤ 63
T₁ = h + Σ₁(e) + Ch(e, f, g) + Kₜ + Wₜ
T₂ = Σ₀(a) + Maj(a, b, c)
h ← g   g ← f   f ← e   e ← d + T₁
d ← c   c ← b   b ← a   a ← T₁ + T₂`}
</Formula>

Only `a` and `e` get new words; the other six shift by one place (step 3). FIPS computes the whole
schedule first (step 1). The lab computes `Wₜ` right before round `t` instead: `Wₜ` depends only on
earlier words, so the values are identical. For "abc", `W₁₆ = 61626380`, because the other three
terms are zero.

The constants lab recomputes `K₀ … K₆₃` (§4.2.2) with exact integer cube roots:

across the word. Addition mod 2³² mixes the two worlds through its carries.

## Two rounds, four rounds

Intel's SHA extensions (SHA-NI) pack the state as `ABEF` and `CDGH` (Gulley et al. 2013).
`SHA256RNDS2` runs **two rounds**, taking both `Kₜ + Wₜ` sums from the implicit `xmm0` (Intel SDM
Vol. 2B). `SHA256MSG1` computes `Wₜ₋₁₆ + σ₀(Wₜ₋₁₅)`, a `PADDD` adds `Wₜ₋₇`, and `SHA256MSG2` adds
`σ₁(Wₜ₋₂)`, for four words.

ARMv8 SHA2 (FEAT_SHA256) runs **four rounds** per instruction: `SHA256H` returns the new `a … d`,
`SHA256H2` the new `e … h`; `SHA256SU0`/`SU1` extend the schedule (Arm ARM).

Both compute schedule words **ahead** of their rounds, in a sliding window of 16 words. The lab
shows clang `-O2` x86 output (as on the [AES-NI page](../../symmetric/aes/aes-ni/)); the
variant picker switches ISAs.

<Lab labId="sha256-sha-ni" producerId="sha256" presetId="sha-256-abc" variant="x86_64-sha-ni" startAt="op:round" layout="instructions:50|registers:50" />

`SHA256RNDS2` reads only the low two words of `xmm0`; a `PSHUFD` then moves the next two sums down
(Gulley et al. 2013). For "abc", `K₁ + W₁ = K₁ = 71374491`, because `W₁ = 0`.

## Nothing up my sleeve

Constants from a short public rule, like ∛2 for `K₀` (FIPS 180-4, §4.2.2), leave a designer
little room to plant weak ones: "nothing up my sleeve" numbers. FIPS states no rationale.

Without the feed-forward (step 4), every round could be undone: the new `b, c, d, f, g, h` are the
old `a, b, c, e, f, g`, and from them follow `T₂`, `T₁ = a − T₂`, the old `d` and the old `h`
(§6.2.2, step 3). Mallory could run blocks backwards from a digest.

Attacks target **reduced-round** versions: Mendel, Nad and Schläffer (EUROCRYPT 2013) give a
collision attack on **31 of 64** steps; full SHA-256 has no known collision.

<Lens level="cryptographer">

Keyed by the message block, the 64 rounds form the block cipher SHACAL-2 (Handschuh & Naccache
2001); step 4 is its Davies–Meyer feed-forward. A public rule still leaves choices
(Bernstein et al., SSR 2015).

</Lens>

</LessonSection>
